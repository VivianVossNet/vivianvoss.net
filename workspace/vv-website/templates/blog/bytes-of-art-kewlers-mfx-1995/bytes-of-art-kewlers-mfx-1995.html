Bytes of Art: 1995 by Kewlers & mfx

A demo from 2006 that dressed itself as 1995 on purpose, and won the room for the one thing that was timeless: the music.

demoscene demo oldschool assembly 2006

Bytes of Art

Here is a small joke that took eleven years to land. In August 2006, at a time when the demoscene was racing itself towards photorealism, two Finnish groups walked on stage at one of the largest computer parties in the Nordics and showed a production called 1995. Not a homage buried in a subtitle. The whole thing was named after a year already gone a decade, and built to look the part. The audience laughed, then it stopped laughing, because somewhere around the second minute the music took over.

The Work

1995 is a PC demo, the joint work of Kewlers and mfx, two Finnish groups who pooled their people for it. Kewlers were the scene's licensed troublemakers: a crew whose logo was a stylised raised middle finger and whose unofficial motto, printed without irony and meant with plenty, was “Kewlers suck”. They had real pedigree behind the swagger, with the demo Variform taking a fistful of scene awards back in 2002. 1995 was announced as their farewell. They released it, took their bow, and went quiet for a few years before drifting back, as demosceners tend to.

The Category

A word on what kind of production this is, because it matters. The scene splits its competitions largely by size. An intro is capped, at sixty-four kilobytes or four or fewer, and the whole art of it is fitting a world into a keyhole. A demo carries no such cap; it may be as large as it likes. 1995 is a demo, and that is the first quiet joke of it. Given an unlimited budget, every byte they could want, Kewlers and mfx spent it dressing up as a machine from eleven years earlier. The constraint here was not the kilobyte. It was the calendar. They tied one hand behind their own backs on purpose, and reached for a palette the rest of the field had spent a decade trying to leave behind.

The Look, and an Honest Note

What comes out is a warm, slightly cheesy tour of mid-nineties demo effects: starfields, a touch of raytracing, a lovely object-pixelate-extrude-heightmap trick, the sort of thing a 1995 machine would have sweated to produce. And here is where a reporter has to be honest, because the scene itself was, in its own comments, cheerfully divided about it. Some found the oldschool feeling pitch-perfect. Others noted, not unkindly, that it was not really very 1995, except perhaps for the credits, and asked whether the style had not already been oldschool in 1995 to begin with. Both camps were right, which is the nature of the thing. Nostalgia is never a photocopy of a year; it is a feeling about a year, and a feeling is allowed to take liberties. 1995 is less a reconstruction than a love letter, and love letters exaggerate.

The Soundtrack

Whatever anyone thought of how faithful the visuals were, the argument stopped at the music. The soundtrack, its main stretch by the Finnish musician Little Bitchard and its closing part by 1in10, is the thing everyone agreed on: techno with vocals that had no business working and worked completely, a set that got better the longer it ran until the credits arrived and, in the scene's own phrase, touched a nerve.

It is worth sitting with the shape of that. A production that spent its freedom looking backward was carried, in the end, by the one element with no era at all. The effects were a costume. The music was the person wearing it.

The Event

It was shown at Assembly, held in Helsinki in early August 2006, one of the oldest and largest demoparties in the world. In the combined demo competition it came third. Then the wider scene had its say: at the Scene.org Awards, the community's own yearly honours, 1995 won Best Soundtrack, and it won the Public Choice award, the one voted by everybody rather than a jury. A group that signed its work “Kewlers suck” left the stage holding the prize the whole room chose to give it.

The Point

The hard days of the week make an argument out of subtraction: fewer dependencies, fewer bytes, fewer moving parts. 1995 makes the gentler version of the same case. Its subtraction was of years. It gave up a decade of graphical progress on purpose, for the fun of a poorer and friendlier palette, and it was rewarded for the least technical thing it contained.

Reduction does not only shrink a program. Sometimes it just picks an older, warmer place to stand, and lets the music do the talking.

The details behind 1995

Authors: Kewlers and mfx, two Finnish groups working together. Code: 216, Curly Brace, Pommak, Uncle-X. Graphics: Droid, Lum, Zomb. Music: Little Bitchard (main part), 1in10 (end part).

Class: demo, which carries no size cap, as against an intro capped at sixty-four kilobytes or fewer. Platform: Windows, OpenGL. Released 5 August 2006.

Shown: third in the combined demo competition at Assembly 2006 in Helsinki. Awards: Best Soundtrack and Public Choice at the Scene.org Awards.

The demoscene has been UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2020 in Germany, Finland and Poland.
